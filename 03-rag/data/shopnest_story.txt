Chapter 1: The Garage Idea

ShopNest began in February 2022 in a rented two-room flat in Kondapur, Hyderabad. Ananya Reddy, who had spent four years running operations for a regional grocery chain, kept noticing the same pattern: small sellers in tier-2 cities such as Warangal, Guntur and Nizamabad took orders over WhatsApp, wrote them in paper ledgers, and lost roughly one order in eight to missed messages. Karthik Varma, a backend engineer she had met at a meetup, believed the fix was not a glamorous app but a boring, reliable order pipeline. Together they decided that ShopNest would be a storefront-in-a-box: a seller uploads products, shares one link, and every order lands in a single dashboard with a payment attached.

The first constraint was money. The founders had Rs. 18 lakh of seed capital from two angel investors, enough for about eleven months if they hired nobody. Karthik therefore chose the most conservative stack he knew. The backend would be a single Spring Boot 2.7 application on Java 11, packaged as one jar, talking to one PostgreSQL 13 database. The frontend was a plain React single page app served by Nginx on the same machine. Everything ran on a single AWS EC2 t3.medium instance with 4 GB of RAM, and the database lived on a small RDS db.t3.micro to avoid the risk of corrupting data on a self-managed disk. Monthly infrastructure cost was Rs. 6,400.

The team wrote down three rules in a file called PRINCIPLES.md and pinned it in the engineering channel. Rule one: every order must be recoverable, so nothing may be deleted, only marked inactive. Rule two: money is always stored as integer paise in a BIGINT column, never as a floating point number, after Karthik watched a colleague at a previous company lose a day to a rounding bug. Rule three: every external call must have a timeout, because a missing timeout turns a slow partner into your own outage.

The first data model was deliberately small. A sellers table held business details and a hashed password. A products table held name, description, price in paise, stock count and a seller identifier. An orders table held the buyer phone number, status, total in paise and a created timestamp, and an order_items table linked orders to products with quantity and unit price captured at purchase time. Capturing the unit price at purchase time was an early lesson: when a seller edited a price, older orders must not change retroactively.

Authentication was equally simple. Sellers signed in with a phone number and password, and the server issued a JSON Web Token valid for twelve hours. Buyers did not need accounts at all; they entered a phone number, received a six digit one time password by SMS, and ShopNest remembered them for thirty days using a signed cookie. This choice removed a whole class of friction, and in the first month it lifted checkout completion from 41 percent to 63 percent compared with the competitor the founders had tested.

By the end of April 2022 the product had nineteen sellers, mostly saree shops, spice merchants and a family bakery in Karimnagar. The first real order came on a Tuesday at 4:12 in the afternoon, a 900 gram pack of Guntur chillies for Rs. 310. Karthik remembers staring at the row in the orders table for almost a minute. The system had no monitoring and no alerts, so he checked the logs by hand every few hours, which worked only because there were so few orders to worry about.

Growth was slow but steady. By June the platform handled about 40 orders a day, and by July about 130. The founders hired their first employee, a junior developer named Sahana Iyer, who joined on a salary of Rs. 7 lakh per year and spent her first week simply reading the codebase. She asked a question nobody had considered: how would they know if the database was slow? Karthik realised they had no answer, because the application logged nothing about query timing.

Logistics turned out to be the second hardest problem after payments. ShopNest did not ship anything itself, so each seller arranged a courier, and early buyers kept asking where their parcel was. Karthik added a simple tracking page that accepted a courier name and tracking number from the seller and displayed a status timeline. It was crude, but the number of where is my order phone calls fell by 35 percent in the first fortnight, and it taught the founders that a visible status is often worth more than a faster delivery. Ananya also began a habit of calling one seller every Friday evening, a ritual she kept for years because it surfaced problems no dashboard could.

That gap would matter sooner than anyone expected. As more sellers uploaded larger catalogues, the dashboard pages that listed products and orders began to feel sluggish, and a pattern in the slow query log suggested that the simple monolith was already hiding a performance problem inside its tidy JPA entities, which is where the story continues in Chapter 2.

Chapter 2: The First Monolith Shows Its Cracks

By August 2022 the ShopNest monolith contained five logical modules inside one Maven project: catalog, orders, sellers, notifications and reporting. The modules shared a single PostgreSQL schema and, more importantly, a single HikariCP connection pool configured with a maximum of 10 connections, which was the library default that nobody had ever questioned. Sahana's question about database speed led Karthik to switch on the slow query log with a threshold of 200 milliseconds, and he enabled the Hibernate statistics endpoint in the staging profile. The results were humbling. The seller dashboard, which listed the last 50 orders with their items and product names, was issuing 151 SQL statements for every page load.

This was the classic N+1 select problem. The Order entity had a lazy collection of OrderItem objects, and each OrderItem had a lazy reference to a Product. The service method loaded 50 orders with one query, then touched the items of each order, producing 50 more queries, then touched the product of each item, producing roughly another 100 queries. In development with five orders the page felt instant, which is exactly why the bug survived. In production a seller with 4,000 historical orders paid a visible price: the 95th percentile response time of the dashboard endpoint stood at 780 milliseconds.

The fix took one afternoon. Karthik replaced the lazy traversal with a single JPQL query using JOIN FETCH for items and products, added a DTO projection for the listing view so that only the needed columns were selected, and introduced pagination with a hard cap of 100 rows per request. The statement count dropped from 151 to 3, and the 95th percentile fell from 780 milliseconds to 240 milliseconds. He also added a small integration test that used a statement counter and failed the build if any controller endpoint exceeded 10 queries, turning a one time fix into a permanent guardrail.

A second problem surfaced in the same review. The orders table had grown to 210,000 rows but had only a primary key index. The query that found all orders for a seller in a date range performed a sequential scan every time. Adding a composite index on seller_id and created_at, in that order, turned a 1.9 second query into a 12 millisecond query. Karthik wrote a short internal note explaining why column order matters in a composite index: the leading column must be the one used for equality filtering, and the second column serves the range condition. The note became the first page of the team wiki.

Reporting caused a third incident. Sellers could export a monthly CSV, and the original implementation loaded every matching order into memory as a list of entities before writing the file. When a bakery with a large wedding season requested a six month export, the JVM, which had been given only a 1 GB heap, threw an OutOfMemoryError and the whole application restarted, interrupting every other seller for about ninety seconds. The team rewrote the export to stream rows with a scrollable cursor and to write directly to the response output stream, so memory usage stayed flat no matter how large the export grew.

These episodes taught the team a habit that survived every later architecture change: measure first, then change one thing at a time. They created a simple performance budget document. A listing endpoint must answer within 300 milliseconds at the 95th percentile, a write endpoint within 500 milliseconds, and no request may hold a database connection for more than two seconds. The budget was enforced by a Micrometer timer on each controller and by a nightly load test that replayed anonymised production traffic against staging using Gatling.

Operationally the monolith was still pleasant. A deployment meant copying one jar and restarting one service, which took 40 seconds. Rollback meant restoring the previous jar. Karthik later admitted that this simplicity was worth more than any architectural elegance during the first year, because it let two engineers ship six times a week without a release manager.

The team also learned something about configuration in this period. Several settings, including the connection pool size, the JWT expiry and the SMS provider key, were hard coded in properties files committed to the repository. After a staging password was accidentally pushed to a public fork, Karthik moved every secret into AWS Systems Manager Parameter Store and made the application read them at startup through Spring Cloud configuration. The change took half a day and removed an entire class of embarrassing mistakes. A rule was added to the pull request template asking whether the change introduced any new setting and where it was documented.

Yet the founders knew that tidy performance fixes would not protect them from the next kind of failure, which involved real money moving between strangers. In September 2022 ShopNest signed up for a payment gateway, and the integration that was supposed to take two days introduced the most frightening bug of the company's early life, described in Chapter 3.

Chapter 3: Payments, Trust and the Double Charge

ShopNest chose Razorpay as its payment gateway in September 2022 because it supported UPI, cards and net banking through a single integration and offered a sandbox with realistic failure modes. The integration looked simple. The server created an order in Razorpay, the React app opened the checkout widget, and after payment the gateway called a ShopNest webhook to confirm success. Karthik assigned the work to Sahana with a two day estimate and a review checklist that included timeouts, retries and signature verification.

The first design flaw appeared in the retry logic. The checkout call to create a Razorpay order used a RestTemplate with a 30 second read timeout, and the code retried up to three times on any exception. Gateways occasionally respond slowly under load, so a call that timed out on the ShopNest side might still have succeeded on the Razorpay side. On the evening of 14 October 2022, during a modest festival promotion, a slow response caused the retry to create a second payment attempt for the same cart. Thirty seven customers saw two debits for a single order, and the support phone number received 61 calls in two hours.

Karthik froze new checkouts, issued manual refunds through the Razorpay dashboard within three hours, and wrote a blameless post incident report the next morning. The root cause was not a bug in any single line of code. It was the absence of idempotency. An idempotent operation produces the same result no matter how many times it is repeated, and payment creation must always be idempotent. The remedy had three parts. First, every checkout attempt now generated an idempotency key, a UUID stored in a new payment_attempts table with a unique constraint. Second, the create order call sent that key to the gateway as a header so that the provider itself deduplicated retries. Third, the webhook handler looked up the attempt by key and ignored events it had already processed.

Webhook security received equal attention. The original handler trusted any POST to the confirmation URL, which meant a malicious person could have marked an order as paid by sending a forged request. The fix verified the HMAC SHA256 signature that Razorpay attaches to each webhook, using a shared secret stored in AWS Secrets Manager and compared with a constant time comparison to prevent timing attacks. Webhooks also arrive out of order and sometimes twice, so the handler became a small state machine: an order could move from CREATED to PAID, from PAID to REFUNDED, but never backwards, and illegal transitions were logged and rejected.

In November the founders engaged Dr. Meera Rao, an independent application security consultant and former bank auditor, for a two week review. Her report listed eleven findings. Two were rated high: sellers could view orders of other sellers by editing an identifier in the URL, a broken access control problem, and error responses sometimes leaked SQL fragments. She also pointed out that ShopNest never stored card numbers, which kept the company in the lightest category of PCI DSS compliance, and she urged them to keep it that way by never logging full request bodies from the checkout flow.

The access control fix introduced a rule that became part of the code review checklist: every repository method that returns seller data must accept the authenticated seller identifier as a parameter, so that forgetting to filter becomes a compile error instead of a security incident. Sahana implemented the change with a Spring Security method level annotation and a custom permission evaluator, and the team added a test that tried to read another seller's order and expected a 404 response.

The cost of the incident was real: Rs. 1.2 crore of potential GMV was paused for a weekend, and two sellers left. But the transparency of the post incident report won back trust. The company published a short public note, and one of the departing sellers returned within a month. The experience also changed how the team thought about failure. Partners fail, networks duplicate messages, and clocks drift; a reliable system assumes all of this in advance.

Refunds deserved their own design. Sellers could refund a payment partially or fully from their dashboard, and each refund became a row in a refunds table linked to the original payment attempt, with the amount in paise and a reason code. The sum of refunds for a payment was constrained by a database check so that it could never exceed the amount paid. Reconciliation ran every night at 2:00, comparing ShopNest records with the gateway settlement report and writing any mismatch to a discrepancies table. In the first month the job found nine mismatches, all caused by webhooks that had been lost during a deployment, and each was repaired by replaying the gateway event.

Payments were now safe, but customers were increasingly complaining about something else entirely. They could pay reliably yet could not find what they wanted, because the search box returned nothing for perfectly reasonable queries, a problem that occupied the team through the winter and forms the subject of Chapter 4.

Chapter 4: When Search Returns Nothing

In January 2023 ShopNest listed 38,000 products across 410 sellers, and the search box was the main way buyers found them. The original implementation was a single SQL statement using ILIKE with percent signs on both sides of the term, applied to the product name and description. It was easy to write and easy to understand, and it began to fail in obvious ways. A buyer typing mobile cover found nothing because sellers wrote phone case, and a buyer searching for kurta set missed listings titled kurti combo. An analysis of one week of logs showed that 31 percent of searches returned zero results, and those searches almost never led to a purchase.

The team first tried the cheapest improvement, which was PostgreSQL full text search. Karthik added a generated tsvector column, built a GIN index on it, and switched to plainto_tsquery with the English dictionary. Query latency improved dramatically, from about 600 milliseconds to 35 milliseconds at 38,000 rows. Stemming also helped, so that cover and covers matched. But the zero result rate only fell from 31 percent to 24 percent. The English dictionary knew nothing about Hinglish spellings, regional product names such as pesarattu mix, or the brand misspellings buyers typed on small phone keyboards.

Next they tried pg_trgm, the trigram extension, which scores strings by shared three character sequences and tolerates typos. Searching for samsng returned Samsung products, and the zero result rate dropped to 17 percent. Trigram search is a good fuzzy matcher but a poor meaning matcher; it still could not connect mobile cover to phone case. Ananya asked a pointed question in a planning meeting: should they adopt Elasticsearch like every other e-commerce company? Karthik prepared a comparison and the answer was no, for now. Elasticsearch meant a new cluster costing roughly Rs. 55,000 per month, a synchronisation pipeline, and an operational burden that two engineers could not carry. The decision was recorded in an architecture decision record numbered ADR-007.

Instead the team invested in a synonym table maintained by the support staff. Whenever support noticed a failed search, they added a row mapping a buyer phrase to a seller phrase. The query layer expanded each search with its synonyms before hitting the full text index. This manual approach worked surprisingly well at first, reducing zero results to 12 percent, but it did not scale. Each new category brought hundreds of fresh phrases, and the support staff spent four hours a week curating them. Furthermore, synonyms fixed single words while buyers increasingly typed whole sentences such as something warm to wear in Hyderabad winter.

It was Sahana who proposed the idea that would change the roadmap. She had been reading about embeddings, numeric vectors that represent the meaning of a piece of text so that similar meanings sit close together in space. A search for phone case and a product titled mobile cover would produce vectors pointing in nearly the same direction even though they share no words. She built a notebook experiment over a sample of 2,000 products, using a small open source sentence model, and showed that the correct product appeared in the top five results for 83 percent of buyer phrases that had previously returned nothing.

The proposal raised practical questions that the team had to answer before any production work. Where would the vectors live? PostgreSQL offered an extension called pgvector that stores vectors in a column and searches them with distance operators, which fit the existing stack perfectly. How long would embedding 38,000 products take on their modest hardware? Sahana measured about 90 products per second on a laptop CPU, so a full pass would finish in a little over seven minutes. And how would they keep vectors fresh when sellers edited products? A background job could re-embed any product whose updated timestamp changed.

While the team debated search technology, they also studied how buyers actually behaved. Session recordings showed that 58 percent of mobile users abandoned the page after a failed search without trying a second query, while desktop users retried more often. Product titles were another weakness: sellers wrote them in ALL CAPS, stuffed them with keywords, or left them as a bare model number. A title quality score, based on length, capitalisation and the presence of a category word, was added to the seller dashboard with suggestions for improvement. Sellers who raised their score saw an average of 22 percent more views, which quietly improved the data that any future search system would depend on.

Karthik approved a two week spike but attached a condition: nothing would reach production until the system survived the biggest traffic test of the year. The festival season was approaching, and the engineering plan for the autumn was dominated by capacity rather than features, because the company was about to face a surge that would stress every assumption the founders had made, as Chapter 5 describes.

Chapter 5: The Diwali Surge

Diwali week of October 2023 was the first time ShopNest advertised heavily. The marketing budget was Rs. 14 lakh, and the campaign promised free delivery on orders above Rs. 499 for seventy two hours. By then the platform served 1,150 sellers and about 3,800 orders on an ordinary day. The marketing team predicted a threefold increase in traffic. Karthik, remembering the festival promotion of the previous year, insisted on a load test, and Gatling scripts replaying a realistic buying journey showed the system saturating at roughly five times normal traffic. The plan therefore seemed safe.

The prediction was wrong by a wide margin. At 8:00 in the evening on the first day, a popular Telugu influencer shared the sale link, and traffic climbed to eighteen times the normal level within eleven minutes. The first symptom was a rising error rate on the product listing endpoint. The second was more serious: the HikariCP pool, which had been raised from 10 to 20 earlier in the year, had every connection busy and a queue of 300 threads waiting for one. Requests began to time out after 30 seconds, the load balancer marked instances unhealthy, and at 8:23 the site went down completely.

The outage lasted 47 minutes. During the incident the team discovered three compounding problems. First, the homepage executed an uncached query for trending products on every request, and eighteen times the traffic meant eighteen times the identical query. Second, when the cache that existed for category pages expired, hundreds of requests regenerated it at the same moment, a failure pattern known as a cache stampede. Third, the pool size was set without considering the database itself: the RDS instance supported about 170 concurrent connections, and three application instances each configured with a pool of 60 would have exhausted it instantly.

The recovery was a sequence of careful changes. Karthik increased the application instances from three to six, but kept each pool at 40 connections for a total of 240, then immediately realised that exceeded the database limit and reduced the pool to 25 per instance, for 150 in total, leaving headroom for administrative sessions. A better rule, he later wrote, is that the sum of all pools must stay below roughly 80 percent of the database connection limit. The team introduced Redis through Amazon ElastiCache with a 60 second time to live for the homepage and a per key lock so that only one request rebuilt an expired entry while the others served the stale value for a few seconds.

Rate limiting came next. A token bucket filter at the Nginx layer limited each client address to 20 requests per second, and the checkout endpoint received a stricter limit. The order service also gained a bulkhead: a separate thread pool for write operations so that slow reads could never starve checkout. Autoscaling policies, based on CPU above 60 percent for two minutes, now added instances automatically, and a scheduled action pre-warmed four extra instances before known campaign times.

When traffic returned the following evening the system held. Peak load reached 22 times normal, error rate stayed under 0.4 percent, and the 95th percentile of the listing endpoint rose only to 410 milliseconds. Revenue during the seventy two hour window was Rs. 2.6 crore of gross merchandise value, compared with Rs. 31 lakh for the same period the previous year. The outage had cost an estimated Rs. 9 lakh in lost sales, but the recovery earned the team a reputation for resilience among sellers.

Behind the scenes the human response mattered as much as the technical one. During the outage Ananya posted a status message every ten minutes on the ShopNest website and on the sellers' WhatsApp broadcast list, saying plainly what was broken and when the next update would arrive. Support staff were given a script that avoided speculation, and nobody was allowed to promise a restoration time that engineering had not confirmed. Sellers later said this honesty mattered more than the downtime itself. Karthik introduced a game day practice in November, in which the team deliberately killed a database connection pool or a cache node in staging and rehearsed the recovery, so that the next surprise would feel familiar.

The surge also exposed a structural limit. Every one of the six instances still ran the entire monolith, so a bug in the notification module could degrade checkout, and scaling meant duplicating all five modules even though only catalog and orders were under pressure. During the retrospective, Ananya wrote one sentence on the whiteboard: we are paying to scale code that nobody is using. The engineering roadmap for 2024 would revolve around splitting the monolith into independent services, a journey that began with Kafka and is told in Chapter 6.

Chapter 6: Breaking the Monolith with Kafka

In January 2024 ShopNest began the migration the founders had been postponing. The guiding principle, borrowed from a talk Karthik had watched, was to extract services along the lines of business capability and data ownership, not along technical layers. After two whiteboard sessions the team agreed on four services: catalog, order, payment and inventory. Each would own its tables exclusively, and no service would read another service's database. Karthik called this the one hard rule of the migration, because shared tables would recreate the monolith with extra network hops. The first service to leave was inventory, because it had the clearest boundaries and the highest contention during sales.

Services still had to cooperate. When a buyer placed an order, inventory had to reserve stock, payment had to capture money, and the order had to end up either confirmed or cancelled. The team rejected distributed transactions, which are slow and fragile, and chose a saga: a sequence of local transactions in which each step publishes an event, and a failure triggers compensating actions such as releasing reserved stock. The order service acted as the orchestrator and recorded the saga state in an order_saga table with values like STOCK_RESERVED, PAYMENT_PENDING and COMPLETED.

Events travelled over Apache Kafka. The team ran a three broker cluster on Amazon MSK with a replication factor of 3 and min.insync.replicas set to 2, which meant that a write was acknowledged only after at least two brokers had it. The orders topic had 12 partitions keyed by order identifier, so all events for one order landed on the same partition and were processed in sequence. Twelve partitions also capped consumer parallelism at twelve instances, a number chosen with room to grow, since adding partitions later would change the key to partition mapping and break ordering guarantees for in flight orders.

The hardest lesson concerned atomicity between a database write and a Kafka publish. In the first version the order service saved the order and then called the Kafka producer. When the process crashed between the two steps, an order existed with no event, and the saga never started. The remedy was the transactional outbox pattern. Instead of publishing directly, the service inserted the event into an outbox table within the same database transaction as the order. A separate relay process polled the outbox every 200 milliseconds, published unsent rows to Kafka, and marked them as sent. The guarantee became at least once delivery: an event could be published twice if the relay crashed after sending but before marking.

At least once delivery means consumers must tolerate duplicates. Each event carried a unique event identifier, and each consumer kept a processed_events table with a unique constraint. Before applying an event, the consumer inserted its identifier in the same transaction as the business change; a duplicate triggered a constraint violation that was caught and treated as success. This technique, known as an idempotent consumer, echoed the lesson of the double charge from Chapter 3, and the team now spoke about idempotency the way other teams talk about unit tests.

Failures also needed a destination. Messages that could not be processed after five retries with exponential backoff were moved to a dead letter topic named orders.dlq, and a small dashboard listed them for human review. In the first month the dead letter topic received 214 messages, nearly all caused by a single bug in which the payment service rejected amounts with more than two decimal places. Because the messages had been preserved rather than dropped, the team fixed the bug and replayed every one of them without losing an order.

Migration was performed incrementally using the strangler fig approach. An API gateway built with Spring Cloud Gateway routed traffic for each extracted capability to the new service while everything else continued to reach the monolith. Inventory moved in February, payments in April, and the order service in July. At each step the team ran both implementations in shadow mode for a week, comparing results and logging differences before switching real traffic.

Costs and complexity rose. The team now had four deployables, a message broker, and a new category of bug that appeared only when events arrived in unexpected order. Karthik was honest in the retrospective: for a team of eight engineers the microservices had a real price, and he would not recommend them below roughly that size. But the Diwali problem was solved structurally, since catalog could scale to ten instances while inventory ran on three.

The new architecture, however, made one thing dramatically harder: finding out why something was slow or broken. A single order now touched five processes, three databases and a Kafka topic, and the team's old habit of reading one log file no longer worked, which led directly to the investment in observability told in Chapter 7.

Chapter 7: Observability and the First On-Call Rota

When the monolith was split, the number of places a request could fail multiplied. In March 2024 an incident made the problem concrete. Buyers reported that orders stayed in the PAYMENT_PENDING state for several minutes. Engineers opened logs on four services, searched by buyer phone number, found nothing that matched, and spent two hours before discovering that the payment consumer had fallen behind on its Kafka partition by 41,000 messages. Nobody had been alerted, because no metric measured consumer lag.

Karthik declared observability the engineering priority for the quarter and organised it into the three classic pillars: metrics, logs and traces. For metrics, every Spring Boot service exposed a Prometheus endpoint through Micrometer. The team scraped them every 15 seconds with Prometheus and visualised them in Grafana. They standardised on the four golden signals: latency, traffic, errors and saturation. Each service dashboard showed request rate, error percentage, 95th and 99th percentile latency, JVM heap usage, HikariCP active connections and Kafka consumer lag, always in the same layout so that an engineer from another team could read it instantly.

Logging was restructured next. The old plain text logs were replaced by JSON logs including a correlation identifier generated at the gateway and propagated through HTTP headers and Kafka message headers. A filter placed the identifier into the logging context, so every line written while handling a request carried it automatically. Logs shipped to Amazon OpenSearch through Fluent Bit, and searching for a single correlation identifier now returned the complete story of an order across all services in under two seconds.

Distributed tracing came from OpenTelemetry. The Java agent instrumented HTTP calls, JDBC queries and Kafka producers and consumers without code changes, and traces were stored in Grafana Tempo. The first trace of a checkout request was a revelation. It showed that 38 percent of the checkout latency came from a synchronous call to the notification service that nobody needed to wait for. Moving that call to an asynchronous event reduced the 95th percentile checkout latency from 920 milliseconds to 540 milliseconds, an improvement found in minutes that metrics alone would never have revealed.

Alerting required restraint. The first rules fired on any CPU spike, and within a week the on-call engineer was receiving 140 notifications per night and ignoring most of them, a condition called alert fatigue. The team rewrote the rules around service level objectives. They defined that checkout availability must be at least 99.9 percent over thirty days, which allowed about 43 minutes of downtime per month, and they alerted on the error budget burn rate: a page was sent only if the budget would be exhausted within two hours at the current rate. Pages dropped to about three per week, and almost all of them were real.

The on-call rota began in April 2024 with six engineers, each taking one week in turn, supported by a primary and a secondary. Every alert linked to a runbook, a short document with the likely cause, three diagnostic commands and the safe mitigation. The runbook for consumer lag told the responder to check the partition assignment, verify that the consumer group had all members, and if necessary scale consumers up to the number of partitions. After each incident a blameless review recorded a timeline, contributing factors and follow up actions with owners and dates.

One cultural change mattered as much as the tooling. The founders agreed that engineers who were paged at night would start two hours late the next morning, and that repeated pages for the same cause would be treated as a bug to fix rather than a burden to endure. In the following quarter the number of repeated incidents fell by more than half.

Dashboards were designed with the on-call engineer in mind. Each service had a single overview page whose top row showed the service level indicators in green, amber or red, and engineers were taught to read it from left to right, because the first red panel usually identified the cause. A synthetic monitor in a different region placed a test order through the real checkout every minute, using a dedicated test seller and a payment sandbox, and paged the team if three consecutive runs failed. The synthetic check caught a certificate expiry two days before it would have affected buyers, justifying its small cost of about Rs. 900 a month.

Visibility revealed another uncomfortable number. With every service now measured, the team could see how many resources they were paying for but not using, and the monthly cloud invoice had quietly grown to a figure that made Ananya summon an emergency meeting, which is where Chapter 8 begins.

Chapter 8: The Cloud Bill Shock

In May 2024 the AWS invoice arrived at Rs. 9.6 lakh for the month, up from Rs. 3.1 lakh a year earlier, while revenue had grown only about 2.4 times. Ananya called an emergency session and asked a blunt question: where is the money going? Karthik had no answer, because nobody had tagged resources by owner or environment. The first action was therefore administrative. Every resource received mandatory tags for team, service and environment, enforced by a policy that rejected untagged deployments. Within two weeks Cost Explorer produced a ranked breakdown.

The largest line was compute at 41 percent, followed by the managed database at 22 percent, Kafka at 14 percent, data transfer at 11 percent, and a long tail. Investigation of compute showed that staging mirrored production at full size and ran around the clock, although engineers used it only about ten hours a day. A scheduler that stopped non production environments between eight in the evening and eight in the morning, and all weekend, reduced that portion of the bill by 62 percent without any inconvenience, because the environments started again in four minutes from a script.

The database was next. The primary RDS instance was a db.r5.2xlarge with 64 GB of memory, selected during the Diwali panic and never revisited. Metrics showed average CPU of 18 percent and a peak of 44 percent. After a load test confirmed headroom, the team resized it to a db.r5.xlarge, halving its cost, and moved reporting queries to a read replica so that heavy exports could never compete with checkout. They also found that automated snapshots were retained for 35 days for every environment, including staging, and reduced retention for non production to three days.

Data transfer held a surprise. A large share of the data transfer cost came from NAT gateway processing: services in private subnets were downloading container images, calling the S3 API and sending logs over the public internet path. Karthik added VPC endpoints for S3 and the container registry, which routed that traffic privately and removed most of the NAT charges. Cross availability zone traffic from Kafka consumers, billed per gigabyte in both directions, was reduced by enabling rack aware fetching so that consumers read from a replica in their own zone.

Storage and compute purchasing were optimised as well. Product images, which had been stored in S3 Standard for two years, were moved by a lifecycle rule to Infrequent Access after 60 days, because buyers rarely opened old listings. Stateless workers such as the image resizer and the email sender ran on Spot instances at about 70 percent below the on demand price, with a graceful shutdown hook that finished the current job when a two minute interruption notice arrived. Steady baseline capacity was covered by a one year Compute Savings Plan for 45 percent of the on demand compute spend, a figure chosen conservatively to avoid over committing.

Smaller engineering habits also contributed. Docker images, previously 780 MB because they carried a full JDK and build tools, were rebuilt with multi stage builds and a slim JRE base, shrinking to 190 MB, which made deployments faster and cheaper to store and transfer. The CI pipeline on GitHub Actions cached Maven dependencies and ran test suites in parallel, cutting build time from 19 minutes to 7. Deployments used a blue green strategy: the new version started beside the old, passed health checks and a smoke test, and only then received traffic, so a bad release was reverted by flipping the load balancer back.

By August 2024 the monthly bill had fallen from Rs. 9.6 lakh to Rs. 5.8 lakh while traffic continued to grow. The team wrote a one page guide on cost awareness and added a cost dashboard to the weekly engineering review, with each service owner accountable for their line. Karthik summarised the lesson in a sentence that ended up on a sticker: every architecture decision is also a purchasing decision.

Finally the team examined the cost of the observability stack itself. Log volume had reached 380 GB a month, and about half of it was debug output from two chatty libraries. Setting those loggers to warning level, sampling traces at 10 percent for healthy requests while keeping every slow or failed trace, and moving log retention from 90 days to 30 days for non critical services reduced that bill by 48 percent. Karthik pointed out the irony that the tools built to prevent surprises had themselves produced one, and he added a monthly review of telemetry volume to the engineering calendar, owned by a rotating engineer.

With infrastructure stable and spending under control, attention finally returned to the idea Sahana had proposed eighteen months earlier. Embeddings, vector search and a support assistant could now be built on a platform solid enough to carry them, and Chapter 9 describes how that experiment became the most talked about feature of the year.

Chapter 9: Teaching Search to Understand

Work on semantic search started in September 2024 with a deliberately modest scope. The team chose the open source sentence model all-MiniLM-L6-v2 because it was small, about 90 MB, ran acceptably on ordinary CPUs, and produced 384 dimensional vectors, a size that kept storage reasonable. The model could consume at most 256 word pieces per input, so long product descriptions needed to be divided into smaller chunks before embedding. Sahana wrote a chunker that split text into pieces of about 1000 characters with an overlap of 150 characters, always breaking at sentence boundaries so that no sentence was cut in half.

Storage used pgvector inside the existing PostgreSQL cluster. A product_chunks table held the chunk text, the product identifier and a vector column declared with 384 dimensions. The team created an HNSW index with the cosine operator class, a graph based index that trades a little recall for much faster queries, and set the query time parameter ef_search to 80 after experiments showed it gave 98 percent recall against exact search. For the catalogue of 38,000 products, producing about 61,000 chunks, the vector index added 310 MB and typical nearest neighbour queries ran in 6 milliseconds.

The first release replaced the keyword search box with a hybrid approach. The system embedded the buyer query, retrieved the 20 nearest chunks by cosine distance, merged them with the top 20 full text results using reciprocal rank fusion, and returned the combined ranking. Buyers who typed warm clothes for winter now saw sweaters and shawls. The zero result rate fell from 12 percent to 2.1 percent, and revenue from search sessions rose by 17 percent in the first month, a result that justified every hour of the experiment.

The second project was a support assistant built with retrieval augmented generation, usually abbreviated RAG. ShopNest had 140 pages of policy documents covering returns, refunds, delivery windows and seller payouts, and support staff answered the same fifty questions repeatedly. The assistant embedded the buyer question, retrieved the three most similar policy chunks, and sent them together with the question to a large language model with an instruction to answer only from the provided context. The retrieval step supplied the facts, and the language model supplied the fluent wording.

The pilot began with an embarrassing failure. In its first week, the assistant told a buyer that returns were accepted within 30 days for all items, which was false: the real policy was seven days, and non returnable for perishable goods. Investigation showed that the relevant chunk had been split in the middle of a sentence and the key sentence about perishables had landed in a chunk that scored lower than three less relevant ones. After the chunker was changed to respect sentence boundaries and to keep 150 characters of overlap, the same question retrieved the correct passage, and the prompt was tightened to say that if the context did not contain the answer, the model must reply that it did not know.

To avoid relying on impressions, the team built an evaluation set of 120 questions, each paired with the policy chunk that contained the answer. They measured hit rate at three, the share of questions for which the correct chunk appeared in the top three results. The initial hit rate was 61 percent. Fixing the chunking raised it to 79 percent, adding the document title to every chunk as a prefix raised it to 84 percent, and a small query rewriting step that expanded abbreviations raised it to 88 percent. The team recorded these numbers in the repository so that every later change could be compared against a baseline.

Quality also depended on discipline around the model's limits. An early version used 4000 character chunks for convenience, and retrieval quality was poor, because the model ignored everything after the first 256 word pieces; roughly three quarters of each chunk was invisible to the search. Switching back to 1000 characters restored accuracy. The lesson, which Sahana put into the project wiki, was simple: measure your chunks in tokens as well as characters, and never trust a pipeline that silently truncates.

Latency and cost were measured too. Embedding a single query took about 14 milliseconds on the application server, and the vector search added 6 milliseconds, so retrieval cost roughly 20 milliseconds in total, while the language model call dominated the response time at around 1.8 seconds. The team cached embeddings of frequent queries in Redis for a day, which removed the embedding step for roughly one query in four. Monthly spend on the language model API came to under Rs. 11,000 during the pilot, a small figure compared with the support hours saved.

The assistant handled 1,900 conversations in its first month and resolved 63 percent without a human, saving an estimated 120 support hours. Yet the team was aware that the system was still a prototype, assembled by a handful of engineers who had learned as they went. Success had created a new challenge, which was growing the organisation itself while keeping its engineering culture intact, the subject of the final chapter.

Chapter 10: Growing the Team and Looking Ahead

By the start of 2025 ShopNest employed 31 people, up from four three years earlier, and engineering made up 14 of them. The company closed a Series A round of Rs. 42 crore led by a Bengaluru venture fund in March 2025, and the founders faced the problems that every successful startup meets: hiring, process and the temptation to rewrite things that were working. Karthik wrote an engineering handbook in the first quarter of 2025 describing how the team made decisions, and the most important page was the one about writing down architecture decisions as short documents with context, options considered, the decision and its consequences.

Hiring followed a consistent pattern. Candidates received a take home exercise of about three hours, typically a small service with an intentional performance flaw, and the follow up interview reviewed their solution together instead of asking trivia questions. The team valued engineers who explained trade offs honestly. Sahana, now a senior engineer, led onboarding and created a first week checklist: run the whole platform locally using Docker Compose, fix a labelled starter bug, shadow one on-call shift, and present one thing that confused them to the team. That last item produced dozens of documentation fixes in a year.

The team also confronted technical debt in a structured way. Every quarter they listed the debts that slowed delivery, estimated the cost of each in engineer days per month, and committed 20 percent of capacity to paying down the top three. The legacy monolith, which still handled seller onboarding and reporting, was the largest item. Rather than rewriting it, they decided to extract seller onboarding in the third quarter of 2025 and leave reporting inside the monolith until its traffic or change rate justified the cost.

Security matured alongside the team. Dr. Meera Rao returned for an annual review and found that the high severity findings from 2022 had not recurred, although she recommended adding dependency vulnerability scanning to the build, rotating secrets automatically every ninety days, and running a tabletop exercise for a data breach. The company adopted all three, and later obtained an ISO 27001 certification, which unlocked contracts with two larger retail partners.

The roadmap for 2025 and 2026 was ambitious but bounded. First, expand the support assistant into a seller copilot that could draft product descriptions, answer questions about payouts and flag suspicious orders, while keeping every answer grounded in retrieved documents and logging each retrieval for audit. Second, introduce multi region deployment so that a failure of the Mumbai region would not take the platform offline, with a target recovery time of 15 minutes and a recovery point of under one minute for order data. Third, reduce the cost of serving a thousand orders by 30 percent through caching, batching and smarter scaling.

Looking back, the founders drew five lessons. Start with the simplest architecture that can work and let measured pain justify every added component. Treat money as sacred: make every payment path idempotent and every state transition explicit. Invest in observability before you need it, because you cannot fix what you cannot see. Treat cost as an engineering metric, not an afterthought. And adopt new technology such as vector search only when you can measure whether it helped, with an evaluation set and a baseline.

Ananya often told new hires the story of the first order, the packet of Guntur chillies that sold for Rs. 310 on a quiet Tuesday afternoon. Three years later the platform processed more than 41,000 orders on an ordinary day, supported 6,200 sellers across 188 towns, and ran at 99.95 percent availability for the previous quarter. The numbers were far beyond anything the founders had imagined in that rented flat in Kondapur, yet the principles in PRINCIPLES.md had not changed at all.

Community and culture received deliberate attention. ShopNest ran a seller advisory council of twelve merchants who met quarterly with the product team, and several roadmap items, including the bulk upload tool and the festival calendar, came directly from those sessions. Internally, the company held a monthly demo day where any engineer could show unfinished work, and a quarterly learning week during which the whole team stopped feature work to study a topic of their choice. The most popular learning week was devoted to distributed systems failure modes, and it produced three new game day scenarios and an updated runbook for Kafka broker loss.

The next chapter of ShopNest has not been written yet, and the team intends to write it the same way as the first nine: carefully, measurably and one decision at a time.
